Prime Rate Is 4.70%. What It Means for Your Variable Mortgage

Published November 6, 2026 · Updated November 6, 2026 · 4 min read

Short answer: if you have a variable-rate mortgage, a HELOC or a prime-based line of credit, your rate went up a quarter point on October 29, 2026. Whether your monthly payment changes depends on your mortgage type, so check your contract and ask your lender for the new numbers in writing.

4.70%
Big Six banks’ prime rate since Oct 29, 2026, up from 4.45%

The Bank of Canada raised its policy rate to 2.50% on October 28, its first hike since 2023, and the big banks followed the next day. A quarter point sounds small, but it reaches every loan tied to prime. This is part of The Weekly Economy, our weekly Canadian money brief.

What Changed This Week

  • October 28: the Bank of Canada raised its policy rate by 0.25 points to 2.50%, saying it wants to stop high energy prices from spreading to other goods and services.
  • October 29: the six biggest banks raised their prime rate from 4.45% to 4.70%.
  • TD also has a separate mortgage prime rate, reported at 4.85%. Check your own lender’s posted rate, because reports differ on some figures.
  • What’s next: the Bank said it could need to raise rates again. Its next decision is December 9, 2026.

Who Is Affected

  • Variable-rate mortgage with an adjustable payment: your payment goes up with prime.
  • Variable-rate mortgage with a fixed payment: your payment stays the same, but more of it goes to interest, so your balance falls more slowly. If your payment barely covers the interest, you may be near your trigger rate, the point where it no longer does.
  • Home equity line of credit (HELOC) or a prime-based line of credit: the rate normally moves with prime. Check your agreement.
  • Fixed-rate mortgage: your rate stays the same until your term ends.

Rough math: a quarter point on a $400,000 balance is about $1,000 a year in extra interest, or roughly $83 a month.

What to Check This Week

  1. Find your mortgage type in your contract or lender statement: fixed rate, variable with fixed payment, or variable with adjustable payment.
  2. Ask your lender in writing: your new rate, your new payment, your trigger rate, and the date the change takes effect.
  3. Check your lines of credit and any other prime-based loans for the same change.
  4. Plan for more: another increase on December 9 is possible. Budget for it now rather than later.
  5. Renewing within a year? Ask for written quotes from your lender and at least one other.

If Your Bank Gets It Wrong

Wrong rate, wrong payment, or an answer you can’t get? Complain in this order:

  1. Write to your bank and get a reference number.
  2. Ask it to escalate to its complaint-handling office, such as the TD Ombudsman or the RBC Client Complaints Appeal Office.
  3. Wait for the final response. Banks have up to 56 days.
  4. Go to OBSI, free, at 1-888-451-4519, within 180 days of the final response, or if 56 days have passed.

Frequently Asked

Will my payment go up because of the 4.70% prime rate?

It depends on your mortgage. With an adjustable payment, yes, your payment rises with prime. With a fixed-payment variable mortgage, your payment stays the same but more goes to interest. With a fixed-rate mortgage, nothing changes until your term ends.

What is a trigger rate?

It is the rate at which your fixed payment no longer covers the interest on a variable mortgage. Ask your lender for your trigger rate in writing.

Will the Bank of Canada raise rates again?

It has not said. The Bank said it could need to raise the policy rate further. Its next decision is on December 9, 2026.

Does my line of credit change too?

A prime-based line of credit or HELOC normally moves with prime, so its rate went up on October 29. Check your agreement or ask your lender.

Where do I complain if my bank handles this badly?

Complain to your bank in writing first. If it hasn’t resolved the issue within 56 days, or you disagree with its final answer, go to OBSI for free at 1-888-451-4519.
