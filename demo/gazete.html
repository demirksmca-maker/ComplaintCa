Markets · Interest rates

The TSX has its worst week since 2020. A December rate hike looks less likely.

A tech and metals sell-off pushed the TSX down about 8% in a week and into a correction. On Monday, inflation held at 3.1% and price pressure under the surface eased, so markets cut the odds of a Bank of Canada hike on December 9 to about 40%.

Economy Desk · November 23, 2026 · Sources: Statistics Canada, The Canadian Press, BNN Bloomberg, The Globe and Mail

The TSX closed Friday at 33,189.85 after seven straight losing days, down 8.8% since November 11. Technology and base-metal stocks led the drop as investors questioned how much the AI and data-centre boom will really earn. Copper briefly fell below US$12,000 a tonne. In New York, the S&P 500 and the Dow also fell into corrections and the Nasdaq gave back its gains for 2026.

At home, Statistics Canada said prices rose 3.1% from a year earlier in October, the same as September and below the 3.2% economists expected. Gasoline was up 30.9% from a year ago, while cell phone plans got cheaper and rent rose more slowly. Without energy, prices rose 2.0%. That softer picture is why rate-hike bets fell. The policy rate stays at 2.50% until at least December 9.

Homes

Home sales down 10.6% from a year ago

37,827 homes sold in October. The average price was $678,584, down 1.6%.

Shopping

Mostly gasoline. An early estimate says sales fell about 1.5% in October.

What it means for you

A red week is not a reason to make a rushed money decision.

If your RRSP or TFSA dropped, check when you actually need the money before you change anything, and talk to your adviser. Variable rate? Prime is still 4.70%, and a December hike is now less likely, not ruled out. If an adviser or bank gave you bad advice or hid fees, you can complain for free.

SCAM WATCH

The delivery, the bank, then the “police”. All fake.

Manitoba RCMP say a Spruce Plains woman got a chain of calls on one day: first from someone posing as an online delivery service, then her bank, then the Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre and finally a Minnedosa RCMP officer. Each call made the next one seem real. She was told to put about $4,900 into what turned out to be a Bitcoin ATM. The phone numbers were spoofed.

STEP 1Hang up.

Banks, police and the Anti-Fraud Centre never send you to a Bitcoin machine.

STEP 2Call back yourself.

Use the number on your card or the official website, not the one on your screen.

STEP 3Tell someone.

Scammers tell you to keep it secret. That is the warning sign.

How the chain works

Four calls

  1. A “delivery company” says there is a problem with a parcel.
  2. Your “bank” calls about suspicious activity.
  3. The “Anti-Fraud Centre” says your money is at risk.
  4. A “police officer” tells you to move the money to keep it safe.
Caller ID can lie

Red flags this week

  • A caller ID that shows your bank or the police
  • One call that hands you to another “agency”
  • Being told to keep it secret, even from your bank
  • Payment by Bitcoin machine, gift card or crypto
  • A Black Friday deal from a shop you found in a social media ad
Say this: “I’ll call my bank on the number on my card.” Then hang up.
Already sent money?

Call your bank today. Then report it.

Call your bank on the number on your card and ask it to stop or trace the payment. Report to local police and the Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre at 1-888-495-8501. Keep the receipts, numbers and messages.

Opinion
