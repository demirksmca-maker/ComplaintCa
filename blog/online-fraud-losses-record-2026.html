How the Numbers Break Down

The Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre (CAFC) recorded $704 million in reported fraud losses in 2025, up from $643 million in 2024 — a roughly 300% increase since 2020. Identity fraud, investment fraud, and service fraud (which covers most online-shopping and subscription scams) were the most frequently reported types. The CAFC itself estimates that fewer than 1 in 10 frauds are ever reported, meaning the real national loss figure is almost certainly several times higher.

Why So Few People Report It

Two reasons come up constantly: people don't know reporting is separate from getting their money back, and they don't know the right order to do things in. Reporting to the CAFC doesn't recover funds by itself — but skipping it means the pattern behind a scam (the account, the phone number, the fake storefront) never gets flagged for anyone else.

The Reporting Order That Actually Works

1Contact your bank or card issuer immediately if money already moved — this is time-sensitive, especially for a credit card chargeback.

2Report to the Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre (1-888-495-8501) — this documents the pattern even though it won't recover funds directly.

3File with local police if you have specific loss details — required for some insurance claims and larger cases.

How the payment method changes your recovery chances

Reporting is only half of the job; the other half is contacting whoever moved the money, and the right contact depends on how you paid.

  • Credit card: ask the issuer for a chargeback and dispute the charge in writing. Card networks set their own deadlines, so do this first.
  • Interac e-Transfer or bank transfer: phone your bank's fraud line at once and ask whether the payment can be stopped or recalled. Once funds are accepted, recovery is much harder.
  • Gift cards: keep the card, the receipt and the numbers, and contact the card issuer immediately.
  • Cryptocurrency: report to the exchange or wallet provider you used, and keep every transaction ID.

Whichever applies, keep screenshots of the listing or message, the seller's name or handle, and any payment confirmation. A tidy timeline with dates is what banks and investigators ask for first.

Telling the story in your report

When you file with the CAFC or police, write the story in order: how you first met the seller or message, what you were promised, what you paid and when. Include phone numbers, emails, website addresses and wallet or account identifiers. Investigators link reports through these details, so a short but exact report is worth more than a long emotional one.

Frequently Asked

How much did Canadians lose to fraud in 2025?

The Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre recorded $704 million in reported fraud losses in 2025, up from $643 million in 2024. The CAFC estimates that fewer than 1 in 10 frauds are reported.

In what order should I report fraud in Canada?

First contact your bank or card issuer right away if money already moved. Then report to the Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre at 1-888-495-8501. Then file with local police if you have specific loss details.

Will reporting to the CAFC get my money back?

Not by itself. A CAFC report documents the pattern so it can be flagged for others, but recovery depends on contacting whoever moved the money, such as your bank or card issuer.

Does the way I paid change my chances of recovery?

Yes. With a credit card you can ask for a chargeback. For Interac e-Transfer or bank transfer, phone your bank's fraud line at once, because recovery is much harder after funds are accepted. For gift cards, keep the card and receipt and contact the issuer immediately.
