How to Escalate a School Complaint in Ontario: From the Principal to the Ombudsman

Published October 7, 2026 · Updated November 3, 2026 · 7 min read

Short answer: put it in writing to the principal, then escalate to the superintendent, then the board's Director of Education. Only after the board's process is finished can you complain to the Ontario Ombudsman (online, 1-800-263-1830, or info@ombudsman.on.ca; it is free). If the issue is the principal's own professional misconduct, report it to the Ontario College of Teachers.

Since 2015
Ontario's Ombudsman has been able to investigate school boards. It is the last step, after the school and the board have had their chance.

You raised a problem with the principal and nothing changed, or the answer was only verbal. Ontario gives parents a clear ladder to climb. Each step works best when the one before it is in writing.

Step by Step

1Put it in writing to the principal. Email the principal with dates, what happened, and what you are asking for. Ask for a written reply. A verbal promise is hard to rely on later.

2Use the law on notification. If your child was harmed by bullying, a threat, an assault or a similar activity, section 300.3 of the Education Act requires the principal to tell you what happened, the harm, and the steps taken to keep your child safe. Name the section in your email.

3Go to the superintendent. Every school has a superintendent of education above the principal. Find the name on your school's page on the board website, email them, and copy the principal. Attach your earlier emails.

4Then the Director of Education. If the superintendent doesn't resolve it, write to the board's Director of Education (the board's most senior staff member) or its equivalent senior executive. Follow your board's parent concern procedure. At the TDSB, that is the Parent Concern Protocol (PR505).

5Professional misconduct? A separate path: the Ontario College of Teachers. Ontario teachers and principals are College members. If the problem is their own professional misconduct, incompetence or incapacity, submit a concern online or call 437-880-3000 (toll-free 1-833-966-5588). The College can investigate and refer a member to a discipline hearing, but it cannot change a school or board decision. Give your name and contact details, because the College typically doesn't investigate anonymous concerns.

6Last resort: the Ontario Ombudsman. If the board's process is finished and you are still not satisfied, complain to the Ombudsman online, by phone at 1-800-263-1830, or by email at info@ombudsman.on.ca. It is free. The Ombudsman can review how the board handled your complaint and make recommendations, but it cannot overturn a board decision or issue penalties, and it does not regulate teachers.

Step 5 is only for the teacher's or principal's own conduct. If your complaint is about how the school or the board acted, skip it and go from step 4 to step 6. At every step, ask for a reply date in writing and note it. If the date passes with no answer, follow up in writing before you move on.

What to Ask For in Writing

What to write in your first email or letter:

  • Full names: yours, your child's, the principal's or teacher's, and the school.
  • Dates: what happened and when, one line per event.
  • Earlier contact: who you told, when, and what they said. Attach those emails.
  • What you want: a safety plan, a meeting or a decision, and a written reply by a date.

Then ask for:

  • A written account of what happened and when.
  • A safety plan: what will change, for example separation in class or supervision.
  • Who was told: whether staff reported it to the principal, as section 300.2 requires.
  • Support for your child, such as a school social worker.
  • A meeting, with an interpreter if you need one.
  • Copies of records about your child, under Ontario's access-to-information law (MFIPPA).

Anonymous? The Ontario College of Teachers typically doesn't investigate anonymous concerns, so give your name and contact details when you report misconduct. Send everything by email, so there is a dated record, and keep a log of calls and meetings.

Frequently Asked

Who is above a school principal in Ontario?

The superintendent of education who oversees the school's family of schools. Above the superintendent is the school board's Director of Education. Your school's page on the board website usually lists its superintendent.

Does the principal have to tell me what happened to my child?

Yes, in many cases. Under section 300.3 of Ontario's Education Act, if the principal believes a student was harmed by certain serious activities, such as bullying or assault, the principal must tell the parent the nature of the activity, the harm, and the steps taken to protect the student, including any discipline.

When can I complain to the Ontario Ombudsman about a school board?

After you have used the board's own complaint process. The Ombudsman is an office of last resort and has overseen Ontario school boards since September 1, 2015. You can complain online, by phone at 1-800-263-1830, or by email at info@ombudsman.on.ca.

Can a TDSB trustee help with my complaint?

Not while the board is under provincial supervision. The TDSB says that during supervision the powers of trustees rest with the provincially appointed supervisor, and trustees have no official duties with the board. Escalate to the superintendent and the Director of Education instead.

How do I file a complaint against a school superintendent?

Write to the board's Director of Education, the senior staff member above the superintendent. Attach your earlier emails to the principal and superintendent, follow the board's parent concern procedure, and ask for a written answer. If the board's process ends and it is still unresolved, you can complain to the Ontario Ombudsman.

Can I report a principal to the Ministry of Education?

The Ministry of Education generally sends parents back to the school board first: the superintendent, then the Director of Education. If the board doesn't resolve it, the Ontario Ombudsman can review how the board handled it. If the issue is the principal's own professional misconduct, report it to the Ontario College of Teachers, because Ontario principals are College members.

Which comes first, the Ontario College of Teachers or the Ombudsman?

They do different jobs, so they are not two rungs of one ladder. The College handles a teacher's or principal's own professional misconduct. The Ombudsman reviews how the board handled your complaint, after the board's own process is finished.

What can the Ontario Ombudsman do about my school board?

It can review whether the board handled your complaint fairly and make recommendations. It cannot overturn a board decision or issue penalties, its recommendations are not binding, and it does not regulate teachers.

Can I complain about a principal anonymously?

You can raise a concern without your name, but it is harder to act on. The Ontario College of Teachers typically doesn't investigate anonymous concerns. If a child may be in danger, call the police or your local child protection agency right away.
