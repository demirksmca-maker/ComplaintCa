Spear-phishing isn't the "your package couldn't be delivered" text you get by the hundred. It's personalized, researched, and built to pass your gut check — which is exactly why it's draining more money than ordinary phishing.

October 2026 Update

The CAFC's mid-year data shows spear-phishing losses reached about $57.7 million between January and June, making it the second-largest category behind investment fraud. Total reported fraud losses for January to June 2026 were about $351 million. The CAFC places spear-phishing among the scam types that grew compared with last year.

What's Actually Happening

The Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre has already logged 33,854 online fraud reports so far in 2026. Spear-phishing — fraud that uses real, specific details about you or your employer to seem legitimate — accounts for a disproportionate share of the losses: $31 million CAD in the first quarter alone. For context, total fraud losses in Canada hit $643 million in 2024, nearly 300% higher than in 2020, and the Centre itself estimates only a small fraction of incidents ever get reported.

Recent polling backs up why it's working: 87% of Canadians say it's getting harder to tell whether an online ad is real, and 75% say the same about telling a fake shopping site from a real one.

Spear-Phishing vs. Regular Phishing

Regular phishing is a mass email or text — generic, sent to thousands of people, easy to spot once you know the pattern ("Your account has been suspended, click here").

Spear-phishing is targeted. It might reference your actual employer, a real coworker's name, a project you're genuinely working on, or a supplier you actually use — pulled from LinkedIn, a data breach, or a company website. That specificity is what gets it past the "this looks fake" instinct.

Red Flags — Even When It Looks Legitimate

  • ⚠️ A request to change payment details, wire funds, or buy gift cards — especially if it claims urgency ("before end of day")
  • ⚠️ An email that matches a real contact's name and writing style but comes from a slightly different domain (e.g. an extra letter, a different TLD)
  • ⚠️ A message that references accurate personal or work details but asks you to act outside your normal process
  • ⚠️ Pressure to bypass a second person for approval, or to keep the request confidential

If You Think You've Been Targeted

Haven't sent anything yet? Verify the request through a separate channel — call the person or company directly using a number you already had, not one from the message.

Already sent money or shared banking details? Phone your bank or card company straight away to flag the transaction, then report it — the earlier both happen, the better the odds of recovery.

Protecting a small business from a targeted email

Spear-phishing often lands in accounts-payable inboxes, where a single believable email can move real money. A few habits make the biggest difference.

  • Verify payment changes by phone. If a supplier or executive asks you to change bank details, call a number you already have on file, not the one in the message.
  • Require two approvals for wire transfers and new payees, even when the request appears urgent.
  • Look closely at the sender domain. One extra letter or a different ending is a common trick.
  • Turn on multi-factor authentication for email and banking so a stolen password is not enough.

If a payment has already gone out, call the bank right away and ask whether the transfer can be recalled, then report to the CAFC and local police. Speed is the difference between a recall attempt and a write-off.

The first hour after a suspected click

If you opened a link or attachment in a suspicious message, tell your IT contact immediately and disconnect the device from the network. Change the passwords for the affected account from a different device, then check your mailbox for forwarding rules or sign-in locations you do not recognise, which attackers often create to stay hidden.

Frequently Asked

What is spear-phishing?

Spear-phishing is targeted fraud that uses real, specific details about you or your employer, such as a coworker's name or a supplier you use, to look legitimate. Regular phishing is a generic mass email or text.

How much money is lost to spear-phishing in Canada?

The Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre reports about $31 million lost in Q1 2026 and about $57.7 million from January to June 2026. It is the second-largest category behind investment fraud. Only a small fraction of incidents are reported.

What are the red flags of a spear-phishing message?

A request to change payment details, wire funds or buy gift cards with urgency; a sender domain with one extra letter or a different ending; accurate personal or work details paired with a request outside your normal process; and pressure to skip a second approval or keep it confidential.

What should I do if I already sent money?

Phone your bank or card company straight away and ask whether the transfer can be recalled. Then report to the Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre and local police. The earlier both happen, the better the odds of recovery.
