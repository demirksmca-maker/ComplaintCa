A bad hospital experience in Ontario isn't a rare complaint to have — it's the single largest category the Patient Ombudsman sees. Here's the actual order to escalate one.

What's Actually Happening

Ontario's Patient Ombudsman received 4,886 new complaints between April 2024 and March 2025, a 10% increase over the year before, continuing a steady climb from 4,429 the year prior. Almost 60% of the 2024–25 complaints were related to hospital care specifically, and complaints about obstetrical and gynecological care rose 29% in a single year. A recurring theme in the report: patients who felt unheard, including some who left the emergency room prematurely without a proper follow-up.

The Right Order to Escalate

1Raise it with the hospital directly — Patient Relations or a similar internal office, in writing, is the required first step.

2Escalate to the Patient Ombudsman if the hospital's own process doesn't resolve it — this office reviews hospitals, long-term care homes, home care, and community surgical/diagnostic centres.

3College of Physicians and Surgeons of Ontario if the complaint is about a specific doctor's conduct rather than the institution.

What to Put in Your Complaint

  • Who and where. The patient's name, the hospital, the unit or department, and the dates of the visit or stay.
  • What happened. Short facts in order. Names or roles of staff if you know them.
  • What you want. An explanation, an apology, a review of care, or a change in practice.
  • Proof. Discharge papers, test results, notes you took, and copies of any letters or emails.

Keep a copy of everything you send. If the hospital doesn't fix it, you can contact the Patient Ombudsman at 1-888-321-0339 or online.

Writing a complaint that gets a real reply

Patient Relations offices handle many letters, and the clearest ones get the quickest answers. Keep the tone factual and the story short: where you were, when, who you spoke to, and what should have happened instead. State the outcome you want, whether that is an explanation, a meeting, a change in practice or an apology.

If the patient cannot write because of illness, a family member or support person can usually complain on their behalf with the patient's consent. Keep a copy of the letter and note the date it was sent so that the Patient Ombudsman can see you tried the hospital first.
