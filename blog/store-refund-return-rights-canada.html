Does a Store Have to Give You a Refund in Canada? Holiday Return Rules

Published November 9, 2026 · Updated November 9, 2026 · 3 min read

Short answer: no. No federal or provincial law in Canada gives a general right to return an item just because you changed your mind; the store's return policy decides. Defective items and items that were misrepresented are different: there, you have legal rights whatever the policy says.

Canadian laws that give shoppers a general change-of-mind right to return goods. For an unwanted gift, the store's policy is the rule.

Between Black Friday and Boxing Day, a lot of sweaters, gadgets and gifts go back to the store. Knowing the difference between a store's goodwill and your legal rights saves you from arguing the wrong point at the returns desk.

Changed Your Mind? The Policy Decides

Ontario's government spells it out: its Consumer Protection Act sets refund rules for certain types of agreements, but does not give a general right to return or exchange goods. Other provinces work the same way for ordinary purchases.

Before you buy, check:

  • How many days you have, and whether holiday purchases get extra time.
  • Refund to your card, or store credit only.
  • "Final sale", clearance and open-box exclusions.
  • Restocking fees on electronics or special orders.
  • Whether you need the original receipt and packaging.

Defective or Not as Described? You Have Rights

Defects: provincial sale of goods laws imply that a product will be of acceptable quality and fit for its purpose. A defective item can mean a repair, replacement or refund, even with a "no returns" sign.

Misrepresentation: in Ontario, if a business misrepresents a product, for example by claiming features it does not have, you can ask for a refund. Put the request in writing.

A manufacturer's warranty is an extra promise on top of these rights, not a replacement for them.

Gifts, Gift Receipts and Gift Cards

Gift receipts usually get you an exchange or store credit at the current price, not cash. Ask for one at the till for every gift you buy.

Gift cards: Ontario banned expiry dates on most gift cards on October 1, 2007, and limits the fees that can be charged on them. Other provinces have their own gift card rules, so check with your provincial consumer office if a card has expired or lost value.

When the Store Refuses

  1. Ask for a manager and quote the policy that was posted when you bought.
  2. Put it in writing to customer service, especially for a defect or misrepresentation.
  3. Contact your provincial consumer office: Consumer Protection Ontario (1-800-889-9768), Consumer Protection BC (1-888-564-9963) or the Office de la protection du consommateur in Quebec (1-888-672-2556).
  4. Paid by card for something defective or not as described? Ask your card issuer about a dispute.
  5. Larger amounts: Small Claims Court.

Frequently Asked

Do stores in Canada have to give refunds?

Not for change-of-mind returns. No federal or provincial law gives a general right to return goods; the store's posted return policy decides. Defective or misrepresented items are different.

Can a store refuse to refund a defective item?

A "no returns" policy does not override the implied warranties in provincial sale of goods laws. A defective product can entitle you to a repair, replacement or refund.

Can I return a gift without a receipt?

Only if the store's policy allows it. With a gift receipt, most stores offer an exchange or store credit rather than cash.

Do gift cards expire in Ontario?

Most do not. Ontario banned expiry dates on most gift cards on October 1, 2007, and limits the fees that can be charged on them.

Where do I complain if a store refuses a valid refund?

Your provincial consumer office: Consumer Protection Ontario at 1-800-889-9768, Consumer Protection BC at 1-888-564-9963, or the Office de la protection du consommateur in Quebec at 1-888-672-2556.
